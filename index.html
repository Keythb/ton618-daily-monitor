The buildout is leaving the balance sheet — $90bn-plus of chip debt went looking for Apollo on the day the FT took $20bn off the tenant's run-rate, and the sponsors fell furthest

The WSJ reports three private-credit chip deals at once: Broadcom arranging more than $50bn for OpenAI's custom-chip programme (Nexus) with Apollo and Blackstone among the lenders, targeted to close by year-end; Oracle negotiating with Apollo and Goldman Sachs to fund a 1-gigawatt site through an off-balance-sheet entity that buys the chips and leases them back; SpaceX pursuing $40bn ($10bn bank loans, $30bn investment-grade bonds, Apollo-led) for Nvidia chips, closing in 2027. The cost of the buildout has outrun what the public debt market will carry on a sponsor's own name.

The same afternoon the Financial Times put OpenAI's annualized revenue at roughly $50bn as of September, against the $70bn investors had been citing; the gap is a method mismatch with Anthropic's run-rate, not a sales shortfall. The reaction ran down the capital structure in order: the lender that walked from Michigan rose (OWL +1.81%), the index barely moved (SPXSPX — S&P 500 Index. The benchmark US large-cap equity index. −0.47%), the chipmakers fell (NVDA −2.91%, AVGO −4.35%) and the sponsor that lets a lender own its chips fell most (ORCL −5.58%).

That ordering is the point of the structure. When a third party owns the hardware and the sponsor holds a lease, the sponsor's equity carries the residual on the tenant's revenue; a revision to that revenue prices through the lease before it prices through the chip. The 24 Sep Blue Owl entry scored HIT on the same logic: fourteen sessions after the force-majeure notice, OWL 9.275 is still under its 9.60 pre-notice close.
